The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Winter

Every evening at a quarter past five, Elsie climbed the one hundred and twelve steps of the Harrow Point lighthouse. She counted them out loud, the way her father had taught her, because the sea liked to hear a steady voice. At the top she wound the old clockwork, wiped the salt from the great glass lens, and lit the lamp that had not failed a single night in forty-one years.

The village below called her the Keeper, though the lighthouse had been automated twice on paper and once, briefly, in practice. The machine had lasted three weeks. On the fourth, a fishing boat named the Little Wren came home through a fog so thick the gulls walked instead of flying, and it was Elsie's lamp, not the machine's, that the skipper saw. After that, nobody mentioned automation again.

That winter the letters began to arrive. They came in pale blue envelopes with a harbour-office stamp, and every one of them said the same thing in slightly different words: the lighthouse would be decommissioned in the spring. A new beacon, solar-powered and silent, would stand on a steel pole at the end of the breakwater. It would need no keeper at all.

Elsie read each letter at the kitchen table, folded it into a paper boat, and set it sailing in the rain barrel by the door. By February there was a small fleet.

On the last night of the season, the storm came in sideways. The wind tore slates from the chapel roof and rolled the harbour bins down the high street like dice. Elsie climbed the steps, counting louder than usual, and when she reached the lantern room she found the lamp already burning. Beside it, wrapped in three scarves and a borrowed oilskin, sat the harbour master himself.

"I thought," he said, not quite meeting her eyes, "that someone ought to learn how it's done."

She handed him the cloth for the lens. "Start with the salt," she said. "The sea always leaves more than you expect."

Outside, far beyond the breakwater, a small light blinked back at them. The Little Wren was coming home.
